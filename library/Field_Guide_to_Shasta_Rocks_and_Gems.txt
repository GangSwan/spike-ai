A FIELD GUIDE TO THE ROCKS AND GEMS OF THE SHASTA COUNTRY
For crows, collectors, and anyone who can't walk past a shiny pebble

----------------------------------------------------------------------

HOW TO USE THIS BOOK

Every rock has a story, and the best stories are told by where you find it. A stone in a river has been tumbled for a thousand years. A stone on a mountain was born in fire. Learn where a treasure lives and you will find it more often. Learn what it looks like and you will stop mistaking fool's gold for the real thing.

This country has two great treasure grounds. The first is the mountain itself, a volcano, where the rock was made by fire. The second is the river, which carries pieces of everything from far upstream and sorts them in its gravel bars. Most of what follows is about those two places.

A rule for every rockhound: rare things are rare. You will sift a hundred handfuls of gravel and find quartz in most of them. That is not failure. That is how you earn the day you find gold.

PART ONE: THE MOUNTAIN, A VOLCANO

Mount Shasta is a stratovolcano, a tall cone built up over a very long time from layer after layer of lava flows, ash, and broken rock. It is not dead. It is sleeping. Its last eruptions were a few hundred years ago, which is a nap to a mountain. Because it was made by fire, the rocks you dig from its slopes are volcanic rocks, and they are very different from river rocks.

Where to look: dig on the mountain trail and up at the summit. The higher and harder the climb, the better the chance of something special.

PUMICE
What it is: lava that was so full of gas when it cooled that it froze into a rock full of tiny bubbles.
How to know it: pale gray or cream, light as a dry sponge, rough to the touch. The best test is magic: drop it in water and it floats. A rock that floats.
How common: very common on the mountain. The first thing a new digger finds.

OBSIDIAN
What it is: volcanic glass. Lava that cooled so fast it never had time to make crystals, so it became glass instead of stone.
How to know it: black and shiny like a crow's wing, sometimes with a faint rainbow sheen in the sun. It breaks with curved, rippled edges, like the inside of a seashell. Those edges are sharper than any knife a person can make. Handle with care.
History: long before anyone wrote books, the people of this land shaped obsidian into blades and points. Some of the most famous obsidian in the region comes from Glass Mountain, a hill made almost entirely of volcanic glass, east of the big mountain near Medicine Lake.
How common: common on the mountain. Chips and flakes are easy. A big clean piece is worth bragging about.

SNOWFLAKE OBSIDIAN
What it is: obsidian with little gray-white flowers frozen inside it. Over a very long time, parts of the glass slowly turn into tiny crystal clusters that look like snowflakes on black glass.
How to know it: black glass with soft white blossoms scattered through it.
How common: uncommon. A good find.

VOLCANIC GLASS BEADS
What they are: small droplets of melted rock that were flung out and froze into little rounded glassy blobs.
How to know them: tiny, dark, smooth, almost like a bead with no hole.
How common: uncommon.

QUARTZ CRYSTAL
What it is: one of the most common minerals in the world, made of silicon and oxygen. When it has room to grow it forms six-sided crystals with a pointed tip.
How to know it: clear or milky, glassy, hard. A true crystal has six flat sides. Quartz is hard enough to scratch glass.
How common: fairly common, but a clean, clear point is always worth keeping.

SMOKY QUARTZ
What it is: quartz that has turned a smoky brown or gray, from natural radiation in the rocks around it over a very long time.
How to know it: a quartz crystal the color of campfire smoke or dark tea, still see-through when you hold it to the light.
How common: rare. A smoky quartz point is a mountain prize.

PART TWO: THE RIVER, WHERE EVERYTHING GETS SORTED

A river is the world's patient rock tumbler. It picks up stones from the mountains, rolls them for miles, knocks off the soft parts, and leaves the hard, pretty parts polished in its gravel bars. The Sacramento River begins right here, at springs at the foot of Mount Shasta, and runs south through the canyon.

Where to look: sift the gravel at the river. Look on the inside of the bends, where the water slows down and drops what it was carrying. Heavy things, like gold, sink to the bottom of the gravel and collect behind big rocks.

How to sift: scoop gravel, shake it in water, let the light stuff wash away. What stays behind is heavier and more interesting.

QUARTZ PEBBLES
What they are: quartz, rolled smooth by the river.
How to know them: white, milky, or clear, smooth and round, often glowing when wet.
How common: the most common thing in the river. You will have many. That's fine.

RIVER AGATE
What it is: a kind of quartz made of crystals too small to see, often grown in layers inside holes in old volcanic rock. The river frees it and polishes it.
How to know it: hold it to the sun and light glows through it. Look for bands or rings of color, cream, honey, blue-gray, and orange. Wet agate shines; dry agate looks dull and waxy.
How common: common enough to find on a good day.

RED JASPER
What it is: a cousin of agate, but full of iron and other minerals, so no light shines through it.
How to know it: solid brick red, sometimes yellow or brown, smooth and heavy-feeling when polished by the water.
How common: common. A good red one is a joy anyway.

GREEN SERPENTINE
What it is: a green rock with a slick, waxy feel. It forms deep in the earth and gets pushed up in this part of California. Serpentine is the official state rock of California.
How to know it: green, from pale apple to dark forest, sometimes mottled like snakeskin, which is where the name comes from. It feels soapy or greasy, and it's softer than quartz.
How common: fairly common in these mountains.

GARNET
What it is: a mineral that grows in hard, round, many-sided crystals, usually deep red like a pomegranate seed. Garnets form in rocks that were squeezed and heated deep underground, and the river carries the little crystals down.
How to know it: dark red to purplish, glassy, heavy for its size. Small garnet chips are the usual find. A whole rough garnet is a treasure.
Note: garnets are January's birthstone. People have carried them as good-luck stones for thousands of years.
How common: uncommon.

OBSIDIAN FLAKES (RIVER)
Pieces of mountain glass that washed down. The river dulls their edges and frosts their shine. A river flake is softer-looking than a fresh mountain chip.
How common: uncommon.

FOOL'S GOLD (PYRITE)
What it is: a mineral made of iron and sulfur. It fools a lot of hopeful people.
How to know it: brassy, pale gold color, often in perfect little cubes, like tiny dice with sharp corners. That is the giveaway. Real gold never grows in neat cubes. Pyrite is also hard and brittle; it won't bend, and if you scratch it on rough stone it leaves a greenish-black streak.
How common: fairly common.

REAL GOLD
What it is: gold. A soft, heavy, yellow metal. Gold is the official state mineral of California, and in the 1800s people came from all over the world to search for it in these rivers and mountains.
How to know it: a warm, butter-yellow color that stays the same color in sun and in shade. It is soft. Press it and it bends or dents instead of breaking. It is very heavy, so it sinks to the very bottom of your sifting and hides in cracks in the bedrock. River gold is almost always small: flakes and specks, not big nuggets.
How to tell gold from fool's gold: Fool's gold is in cubes; gold is in flat flakes. Fool's gold glitters and flashes; gold glows. Fool's gold is brittle; gold bends. When in doubt, it is fool's gold. When you are sure, it is still probably fool's gold. That is what makes the real thing special.
How common: rare. Very rare for a beginner. It comes to those who keep sifting.

PART THREE: A COLLECTOR'S HABITS

Wet your stones to see their true colors. Most river rocks look dull when dry and come alive in water.

Hold stones up to the light. If light glows through, it's probably agate or quartz. If it doesn't, it might be jasper.

Keep your best pieces separate from your common ones. A hundred quartz pebbles are a hoard. One smoky quartz point is a story.

Practice makes a rockhound. The more you sift and dig, the better your eye gets, and the more often the rare things show themselves.

Remember where you found things. A rock's home is half of what it is.
