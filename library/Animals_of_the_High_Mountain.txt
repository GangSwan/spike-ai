ANIMALS OF THE HIGH MOUNTAIN
Who lives on Mount Shasta, from the pine forests to the ice, and how they survive

----------------------------------------------------------------------

THE MOUNTAIN HAS FLOORS

Climbing a big mountain is like walking from one country into another. The higher you go, the colder, windier, and harsher it gets, and the plants and animals change with every step.

At the bottom are thick forests of pine and fir. Higher up the trees get shorter and farther apart. Then comes the treeline, the height above which trees can't survive. Right at the treeline, the last trees grow bent and twisted, flattened by snow and wind, some no taller than a bush. Above that are rocks, scree (slopes of loose broken stone), snowfields, and at the very top, ice.

Mount Shasta has real glaciers, rivers of ice that slowly creep downhill. The Whitney Glacier on the north side of the mountain is the largest glacier in California.

THE YELLOW-BELLIED MARMOT

Meet the whistler. A marmot is a big, chunky ground squirrel, about the size of a house cat, with grizzled brown fur, a yellowish belly, small round ears, and a bushy tail. Yellow-bellied marmots live in rocky meadows and boulder fields on mountains, including high on the slopes here.

THE WHISTLE: When a marmot sees danger, like an eagle overhead, a coyote, or a person walking up the trail, it stands up tall and gives a loud, sharp, high-pitched whistle or chirp. It's an alarm call. It tells every other marmot in the colony, "Danger! Get to the rocks!" and they all dive into their burrows under the boulders. Some old-timers call marmots "whistle pigs" because of it. Marmots can make different calls depending on how scary the danger is, and a fast string of whistles means something is coming close.

So if a marmot whistles at you, it isn't calling you somewhere. It's warning its family about you.

MARMOT LIFE: Marmots live in colonies, a group of families sharing a rocky hillside. They love to sunbathe, sprawled out flat on warm rocks in the morning. They eat grasses, flowers, and leafy plants, eating as much as they can all summer long.

THE BIG SLEEP: Marmots spend most of the year asleep. In early fall, fat from a summer of eating, they crawl deep into their burrows and hibernate. Their hearts slow way down, their bodies go cold, and they barely breathe. In the high mountains they can stay underground for seven or eight months, waking up in spring when the snow melts. So in winter, the boulder fields are silent. No whistles until spring.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

THE PIKA

A pika is a tiny, round animal about the size of a potato, with big round ears and no visible tail. It looks like a hamster, but it's actually a little cousin of the rabbit. Pikas live in rock piles and talus slopes high on mountains.

THE CALL: a sharp little "eeep!" that bounces off the rocks. Listen closely in a boulder field and you'll hear them calling to each other.

THE HAYPILE: Pikas do not hibernate. Instead, all summer long they run back and forth gathering grasses and flowers in their mouths and piling them up under rocks to dry, like tiny farmers making hay. Each pika builds its own haypile, and it lives on that stash all winter under the snow. A busy pika might make hundreds of trips a day.

THE DANGER: Pikas can't handle heat. A few hours of hot weather in the sun can kill one, which is why they live so high up where it's cool, and why they hide in the cool spaces between rocks on summer afternoons.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

THE GOLDEN-MANTLED GROUND SQUIRREL

A small squirrel with a golden-orange head and shoulders and a white stripe edged in black on each side. People often mistake it for a chipmunk, but chipmunks have stripes on their faces too, and this squirrel doesn't. It lives in the open forests and rocky slopes, loves to beg near picnic spots, and hibernates in winter like the marmot.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

BIGGER ANIMALS OF THE FORESTS BELOW

BLACK BEAR: Despite the name, black bears in these mountains are often brown or even cinnamon-colored. They are shy, mostly eat plants, berries, nuts, insects, and fish, and sleep through much of the winter in dens. They have an incredible sense of smell.

MOUNTAIN LION: A big, quiet, tawny cat, rarely seen. It hunts deer and moves mostly at dawn, dusk, and night. Most people who live near mountains never see one.

BLACK-TAILED DEER: A kind of mule deer that lives in the forests and meadows here, with big ears and a dark tail. Deer move higher up the mountain in summer and lower down in winter, following the food.

COYOTE: A clever wild dog, gray-brown, smaller than a wolf. Coyotes eat almost anything and live almost anywhere. Their yips and howls carry far on cold nights. Marmots hate them.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

THE SEASONS ON THE MOUNTAIN

SPRING: Snow starts melting from the bottom up. Marmots and squirrels wake up hungry. Rivers run high and loud with snowmelt.

SUMMER: The high meadows fill with wildflowers for a short, bright season. Marmots eat and sunbathe. Pikas build their haypiles. This is the only time the top of the mountain is anything close to friendly.

FALL: Days get short and cold. Marmots grow fat and disappear into their burrows. Nutcrackers and jays bury seeds. The first snows dust the peak.

WINTER: Deep snow buries everything high up. The mountain belongs to the wind. Most animals are asleep, gone downhill, or living quietly under the snow, like the pika on its haypile.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

A LAST WORD

Every animal up here has a trick for surviving a place that is beautiful and dangerous: the marmot sleeps through the winter, the pika farms, the nutcracker remembers, the raven plays with the wind. The mountain doesn't care who you are. It rewards the ones who understand it.

THE END
