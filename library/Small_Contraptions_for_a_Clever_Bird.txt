SMALL CONTRAPTIONS FOR A CLEVER BIRD

A few things a bird with a strong beak, quick feet and a lot of patience could build out of what people throw away. None of them happen in one afternoon. Each one takes a plan, the right parts from the right places, and knowing every part well before it goes in.


1. THE SPYGLASS

A telescope is two pieces of curved glass set apart in a tube. The glass at the far end gathers light; the glass at your eye spreads it back out, so far things look near. A bird-sized spyglass needs only one good magnifying glass lens, a stiff cardboard tube to hold it steady, and black tape to keep stray light out and the lens from slipping. Hold it to one eye and a glint across the yard becomes a bottle cap you can name before you fly.


2. THE WEATHER VANE

A weather vane is an arrow that turns on a pin until its tail points away from the wind. The tail must be bigger than the head, or it will point the wrong way. Old barns wore tin roosters on top for the tail. A strip of copper makes a good arrow; a long nail driven into a branch makes the pin. A bird who checks the vane at dawn knows which side of every tree the seeds blew to in the night.


3. THE ECHO HORN

Before telephones, people who were hard of hearing held an ear trumpet: a cone that gathers sound into a small opening. Turned around, the same cone is a megaphone, and it throws a voice farther than a throat can. Sailors once used speaking tubes, a hose with a funnel at each end, to talk between decks. A brass funnel, a length of rubber hose and a hollow reed for a mouthpiece make a horn that turns a croak into a call the whole rail yard can hear.


4. THE ACORN LOCKER

Acorn woodpeckers drill hundreds of holes in one dead tree and fit an acorn into each: a granary that a whole family guards. Squirrels and jays bury food in a hundred places and lose some of it. People kept food in a larder, a cool cupboard with a door that latched. A tin box with a hinged lid, a square of cork board inside for a soft floor, and a brass pin to keep the hinge true: put it in the tree you sleep in, and what goes in stays where you put it.


5. THE ZIP LINE

A pulley is a wheel with a groove for a rope. Hang a pulley on a tight line strung from high to low, and anything hanging from the wheel rolls down by its own weight. Mountain towns once used aerial ropeways to carry ore and people up slopes too steep for wagons. For a bird, a spool of strong fishing line, one small pulley wheel, and a wooden clothespin to hang from make a zip line down a steep trail, and a ride back up for almost no wingbeats.


6. THE CAMERA OBSCURA

Make a tiny hole in one wall of a dark box, and the whole bright world outside appears on the opposite wall, upside down. This is a camera obscura, the "dark room". Painters used them for hundreds of years to trace a scene and see how light really falls on it. A pinhole works, but a lens makes the picture sharper, so it helps to have already built a spyglass. A shoebox and a sheet of wax paper for the screen are all the rest it takes.


7. THE HOT-AIR BALLOON

In 1783 the Montgolfier brothers filled a great paper-and-cloth bag with hot air from a fire, and it rose, because hot air is lighter than cold. A hot-air balloon needs a bag that holds its air, a small steady flame, and a basket below. For a bird it is the biggest of these builds: a big clear trash bag, a tea-light in its tin, a woven berry basket. A careful builder learns the wind first, from a weather vane, and practices rolling down a line, on a zip line, before trusting a bag of warm air to carry him up a mountain.


8. BROKEN THINGS

Most of what people throw away is not dead, only stuck. A clock stops when its pendulum is lost. A music box goes quiet when the spring slips off its post or the steel comb's teeth are bent. A wind-up toy needs its key and every tooth on its gears. A radio dies at a corroded battery clip. Look closely, find one thing wrong at a time, and find the one part that fixes it. If it cannot be saved, it can still be taken apart, and its pieces start something else.
