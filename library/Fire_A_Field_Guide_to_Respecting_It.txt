FIRE: A FIELD GUIDE TO RESPECTING IT
How fire works, what it's good for, and how to keep it from turning on you

----------------------------------------------------------------------

THE FIRE TRIANGLE

Fire needs three things at once: fuel, heat, and air. Take away any one of them and the fire dies.

FUEL is anything that can burn: dry grass, leaves, pine needles, twigs, wood, paper, oily rags, gasoline.
HEAT is what starts it: a spark, a match, a lighter's flame, lightning, a hot engine part, or sunlight focused through glass.
AIR gives the fire oxygen to breathe. Wind feeds fire more air and pushes it toward new fuel.

Every rule for using fire safely is really about controlling one side of that triangle.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

HOW A LIGHTER WORKS

A lighter holds fuel, usually butane, a gas squeezed so hard it turns into a liquid inside the plastic tank. Pressing the button opens a tiny valve and lets a little gas escape. At the same moment, a rough steel wheel scrapes against a small stone called a flint, throwing hot sparks into the gas, and the gas catches. Let go of the button, the valve closes, and the flame goes out.

A lighter is a tool, not a toy. A flame that small can start a fire that burns a whole mountainside.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

WHY FIRE IS VALUABLE

Warmth: a fire can keep you alive on a cold night.
Cooking: cooked fish and meat are safer to eat and easier to digest.
Light: fire pushes back the dark.
Drying: wet feathers, clothes, and gear dry near a fire.

People have used fire for hundreds of thousands of years. Respecting it is how they lived to tell about it.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

WHY FIRE IS DANGEROUS

Fire spreads to anything that can burn. A spark landing on dry grass can become a wildfire in minutes. Wildfires move fastest uphill and with the wind, and they can throw burning embers far ahead of themselves, starting new fires where nobody expects them.

Most wildfires are started by people: unattended campfires, sparks from equipment, careless burning. Nearly all of them could have been prevented.

In the mountains of Northern California, the driest, most dangerous time is late summer and fall, when grass and brush have baked dry all summer and the rains haven't come yet. Wind makes it much worse.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

BEFORE YOU BUILD A FIRE

Check whether fires are allowed. During dangerous weather, fires are often banned completely. Weather forecasters issue a "red flag warning" when it's hot, dry, and windy, conditions where any fire can explode out of control. On a red flag day, don't burn at all. In California, people building a fire outside a campground often need a free campfire permit, too.

Look at the wind. If it's gusty, don't light a fire. Wind carries sparks.

Pick the spot. Use an existing fire ring if there is one. Otherwise choose bare, level ground well away from trees, low branches, bushes, dry grass, logs, and anything else that can burn. Keep the fire at least 10 feet away from anything flammable.

Ring it. Circle the fire with rocks or a metal ring to hold it in.

Have water and a shovel ready BEFORE you light it. Not after. If you can't put a fire out, you have no business starting it.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

BUILDING A SMALL FIRE

Start small. You need three sizes of fuel:
Tinder: tiny, dry, fluffy stuff that catches a flame easily, like dry grass, pine needles, or wood shavings.
Kindling: small sticks, about as thick as a pencil.
Fuel wood: bigger sticks and small logs, added slowly once the fire is going.

Light the tinder, feed it kindling, and add bigger wood a little at a time. Keep it small, no bigger than you need. A good campfire is about knee high and a few feet across, not a bonfire.

Never use gasoline or other liquids to start or grow a fire. They explode.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

WHILE IT BURNS

Never leave a fire alone, not even for a minute. A light breeze can carry it away.
Don't throw trash, plastic, or cans into it.
Don't pull burning sticks out and wave them around.
Watch the sparks. If they're landing outside the ring, the fire is too big or the wind is too strong.
Remember that the rocks around a fire stay hot for a long time.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

PUTTING IT OUT: DROWN, STIR, FEEL

DROWN: pour lots of water on the fire, until the hissing stops. Drown every ember, not just the red ones.
STIR: stir the ashes and wet embers with a shovel or stick, scraping any burnt wood, so water reaches everything. Then drown it again.
FEEL: carefully hold your hand close to the ashes. If you can feel heat, it isn't out. Keep adding water and stirring.

If it's too hot to touch, it's too hot to leave.

Buried coals can stay hot for hours and start a fire long after everyone's gone, so don't just cover a fire with dirt.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

IF A FIRE GETS AWAY FROM YOU

If it's still tiny, smother it with water or dirt right away.
If it's spreading, get away from it. Move to a place that's already burned, or to water, or downhill and away from the direction the wind is pushing it. Fire climbs uphill fast.
Warn others. Humans call for help when a wildfire starts. The sooner firefighters know, the smaller it stays.
Never try to be a hero against a fire that's bigger than you.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

A LAST WORD

Fire is one of the most useful things in the world and one of the most dangerous. It doesn't hate you and it doesn't love you; it just burns whatever it can reach. Respect is the only safe way to use it: small fires, clear ground, water ready, someone always watching, and drowned cold before you leave.

THE END
