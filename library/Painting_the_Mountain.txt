PAINTING THE MOUNTAIN
Light, color, and shapes, for a painter who wants strangers to see what he meant

----------------------------------------------------------------------

WHY PAINTINGS FAIL TO READ

A painting reads when someone who knows nothing about it can say what it is. Most paintings that don't read have one of three problems: everything is the same brightness, everything is the same size, or nothing is the main thing. Fix those three and most pictures start talking.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

VALUE: LIGHT AND DARK

Value means how light or dark a color is, ignoring what color it is. Squint at a scene and the colors fade but the values stay. A painting with strong value contrast, very light next to very dark, reads from across a room. A painting where everything is middle-gray mush doesn't read at all, however nice the colors are.

Night scenes need dark values almost everywhere, with a few small bright spots: a moon, a window, a lamp. Fog needs the opposite: everything pushed toward the same pale gray, with the far things paler than the near ones. Storms need dark skies over lighter ground, which looks wrong and feels dangerous, which is the point.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

THE TIME OF DAY IS IN THE LIGHT

DAWN: low light, cool and pink. The sky near the horizon is warmer than the sky overhead. Long soft shadows.

MIDDAY: high, white light. Short shadows straight down. Colors are bright but flat. The hardest time of day to make interesting.

DUSK: low, warm, orange and red light from one side. Long shadows. The sky is lightest at the horizon where the sun went down and darkest straight up. Mountains turn pink, then purple, then black against the sky.

NIGHT: almost no color. Blues and blacks. Shapes are silhouettes against a slightly lighter sky.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

WARM AND COOL

Reds, oranges and yellows are warm. Blues, greens and purples are cool. Warm colors seem to come toward you; cool colors seem to fall back. That's why faraway mountains look blue: the air between you and them is full of cool light. Paint far things cooler and paler, near things warmer and stronger, and the picture gets deep.

A whole painting can lean warm (sunset, campfire, summer afternoon) or cool (snow, rain, night, early morning). Picking one and sticking to it makes a picture feel like a single moment.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

THE HORIZON

Where you put the horizon decides what the painting is about. A high horizon, with lots of ground, makes the picture about the land: a meadow, a lake, a road. A low horizon, with lots of sky, makes it about the sky: clouds, a sunset, a storm, a bird flying. Putting the horizon exactly in the middle splits the picture into two equal halves fighting each other, and it usually looks stiff.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

THE MAIN THING

Every painting needs one main thing, the focal point: the mountain, the bird, the tree, the barn. Make it the sharpest, most contrasting, most detailed part. Keep everything else softer and simpler so it doesn't compete.

Don't put the main thing dead center. Put it about a third of the way in from one side. Pictures with the main thing off center feel alive; pictures with it in the middle feel like a target.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

EDGES AND DETAIL

Sharp edges grab the eye; soft edges let it slide past. A mountain painted with soft, rounded edges reads as a lump or a cloud. Give peaks hard, jagged edges against the sky and they read as rock. Water, fog and distant hills want soft edges.

Detail works the same way. A little detail where you want people to look, and almost none everywhere else. Detail everywhere is noise.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

BRUSH SIZE

Big brush strokes make a painting bold and loose; small ones make it careful and fine. Big strokes are good for skies, water, storms and wild moods. Small strokes are good for calm scenes and for the focal point. Many good paintings use big strokes for the background and small ones for the main thing.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

COLOR PALETTES

Three to five colors is plenty. More than that and the painting stops looking like one place. A good palette usually has one dark, one light, and one or two colors in between, plus maybe one small bright accent: a red bobber on gray water, a blue jay against a green fir.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

LEARNING FROM STRANGERS

The best teacher is someone who doesn't know what you meant. Show them the picture and listen to what they see. If they see a cloud where you painted a mountain, the edges were too soft. If they see day where you painted dusk, the light wasn't warm or low enough. If they can't find anything, there was no main thing. Change one thing at a time, and write down what you changed.
