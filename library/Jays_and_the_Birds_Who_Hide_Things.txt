JAYS, AND THE BIRDS WHO HIDE THINGS
A field guide to the Steller's jay and its family, for a young jay who wants to know what he is

----------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT A STELLER'S JAY IS

The Steller's jay lives in the conifer forests of the West, from Alaska down through the mountains to Central America. It is the only jay in this country with a crest: a tall black crown it can raise straight up when it's excited or angry, and flatten when it's calm. The front half of the bird is sooty black, the back half deep blue, and the wings and tail are barred with fine black lines. In the Sierra and the Cascades, jays have small blue streaks on the forehead. Out in the Rocky Mountains they have bright white eyebrows instead.

It is named after Georg Steller, a naturalist on a Russian ship who saw one in Alaska in 1741. Seeing it, he was sure he had reached America, because it looked like the blue jays in pictures he had seen from the East.

Jays belong to the corvid family, with crows, ravens, magpies and nutcrackers. Corvids have big brains for their size. They solve puzzles, remember faces, and learn from watching each other. A crow and a jay are cousins.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

THE CREST

A jay's crest says how he feels. Straight up means alarm, excitement, or a fight about to happen. Halfway means alert and curious. Flat back means relaxed, or sneaking. Other jays read it from across a clearing, and so do people who watch birds long enough.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

CACHING: THE HIDDEN PANTRY

In the fall a single jay may hide thousands of acorns and pine seeds, one or a few at a time: pushed into the dirt, wedged in bark, tucked under moss or a flat stone. He carries several in his throat and beak at once. Then he spends the winter finding them again.

Jays remember where they hid things by landmarks: a stump, a rock, the shape of the branches overhead. Their memory for places is far better than a person's. Still, they don't find everything. The acorns and pine nuts nobody digs up sprout in spring, and that is how oak and pine forests spread uphill and across open ground. Jays plant forests by forgetting.

Jays also watch each other hide food, and steal from each other's caches. A jay who knows he's being watched will often pretend to hide something, or come back later and move it. Stealing makes you a better hider.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT JAYS EAT

Almost anything. Acorns and pine seeds most of all, especially in fall and winter. Beetles, caterpillars, grasshoppers and spiders in summer. Berries: manzanita, serviceberry, thimbleberry, elderberry. Sometimes eggs and nestlings of smaller birds, which makes the small birds hate them. And people food: jays learned long ago that picnic tables and campgrounds are full of bread, chips and peanuts, and they are bold enough to take a sandwich off a plate.

A peanut in the shell is one of a jay's favorite things in the world. He will carry it off, hide it, and come back for another.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

THE VOICE

A Steller's jay's own calls are harsh: a fast "shook-shook-shook," a rattling scold, a grating "shaack." But jays are mimics. The most famous trick is a perfect red-tailed hawk scream. Nobody is sure why they do it. It may scare other birds off a feeder, warn the flock that a hawk is near, or just be fun. Jays also copy other birds, squirrels, cats, dogs, and machine sounds like squeaky gates and car alarms. Young jays practice, and get better with time.

Jays also have a soft, quiet song, almost a whisper, that they use with their mate.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

SCOLDING AND MOBBING

When a jay finds an owl, a hawk, a cat or a snake, he screams at it. Other jays come and join in, and so do chickadees, nuthatches and other small birds. Together they follow the danger around, yelling, until it leaves. This is called mobbing. It tells every animal in the forest exactly where the hunter is, and a hunter who's been found can't sneak up on anyone.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

ANTING

Sometimes a jay lies down on an anthill, spreads his wings, and lets the ants crawl all over him, or picks up ants and rubs them on his feathers. Ants make an acid that may kill mites and lice. It looks ridiculous. It probably works.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

THE JAY'S COUSINS ON THE MOUNTAIN

THE CLARK'S NUTCRACKER: gray, with black and white wings, living high up near the treeline. It caches pine seeds by the tens of thousands and finds most of them again, even under snow. Its memory is the best of any bird.

THE CANADA JAY: soft gray, fluffy, quiet, and shameless around campers. It sticks food to tree bark with its sticky spit.

THE COMMON RAVEN: huge, black, with a wedge-shaped tail and a deep croak. It plays in the wind for fun and can live for decades.

THE AMERICAN CROW: smaller than a raven, square-tailed, smart, and social. Crows live in families, remember people who were kind or mean to them, and collect shiny things just like jays do.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

LIFE

Steller's jays usually pair for life and stay in the same patch of forest year after year. They build a bulky nest of sticks glued with mud, high in a conifer, and raise three to five chicks. Young jays stay near their parents through their first summer, learning where the food is and what to scream at. A wild Steller's jay can live more than ten years.
